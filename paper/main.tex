\begin{abstract}
Teachers increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to draft exam questions, but the typical
``upload a document, get a quiz'' workflow gives no guarantee that the keyed answer is supported by
the source, sends the material to third-party services, and is rarely evaluated in languages other
than English. We present \Mimir{}, an open microservice system that generates multiple-choice exams
from a teacher's own documents in Hungarian and English. \Mimir{} replaces single-shot
retrieval-augmented generation with a \emph{blueprint} pipeline: a planner allocates concepts,
question types and Bloom levels to slots; each question is written from its own retrieved passages
with chunk citations; and a verifier chain checks grounding, distractors and blind answerability,
feeding failures back to the generator. The system runs on an 8\,GB consumer GPU, keeps uploaded
content only for the duration of a job, and makes human review mandatory before export. We describe
a reproducible evaluation protocol with 12 experiment arms, a 39-document bilingual dataset with 326
human-written reference questions, an LLM judge from a different model family, and a blind teacher
rating. Building the harness already exposed two defects that silently invalidated earlier
measurements: a hard-coded fallback exam that was counted as model output, and a chunker encoder
that truncated every document after 512 tokens---an estimated 68--71\,\% of the average dataset
document. The full comparison of the arms is reported with the protocol described here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Writing good multiple-choice questions is slow: the stem must be unambiguous, exactly one option must
be correct according to the course material, and the distractors must be plausible but provably
wrong. Automatic question generation (AQG) has a long history in education~\cite{kurdi2020systematic},
and general-purpose LLMs have made it accessible to every teacher. The common implementation is naive
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020rag}: split the document, retrieve the top-$k$
passages for the teacher's request, and ask the model for the whole exam in one call.

This design has three weaknesses in a school setting. \emph{Correctness is not checked}: nothing
verifies that the keyed option is supported by the source or that distractors are actually false.
\emph{Control is weak}: the requested number of questions, types and difficulty are only suggested
in the prompt. \emph{Privacy is an afterthought}: teaching materials may contain personal data, yet
they are sent to external APIs and often kept. Evaluation is a further gap: published AQG
results are dominated by English, while our users teach in Hungarian.

\Mimir{} addresses these points with the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{blueprint pipeline} (plan $\rightarrow$ retrieve per slot $\rightarrow$ generate with
citations $\rightarrow$ verify $\rightarrow$ assemble) that guarantees the requested exam shape by
construction and verifies every question before a human sees it (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}).
\item A \textbf{privacy-by-design deployment} on an 8\,GB GPU: zero retention of uploaded content, an
optional local-only mode, a visible processing location and model choice before each upload, and
mandatory human review (Section~\ref{sec:privacy}).
\item A \textbf{reproducible bilingual evaluation protocol}: 12 arms, 39 documents with 326 reference
questions, a cross-family LLM judge, document-level statistics and a blind teacher rating
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item \textbf{Diagnostic findings} from building the harness: two defects that made earlier
end-to-end measurements invalid, and how the harness now detects them (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Surveys of educational AQG~\cite{kurdi2020systematic} note that most systems target English and are
evaluated with reference-overlap metrics or small human studies that rarely check answer
correctness. RAG~\cite{lewis2020rag} grounds generation in retrieved text; graph-based variants build
an entity graph over the corpus to answer questions that need global context~\cite{edge2024graphrag}.
Hybrid retrieval combines lexical BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25} with dense embeddings, typically
fused with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)~\cite{cormack2009rrf}. For Hungarian, multilingual E5
embeddings~\cite{wang2024me5} are a strong open choice, and MILQA~\cite{novak2023milqa} provides
question--answer pairs over Hungarian Wikipedia; EduQG~\cite{hadifar2023eduqg} provides
multiple-choice questions over English OpenStax textbooks. Using LLMs as judges scales evaluation
and correlates reasonably with humans~\cite{zheng2023judge}, but judges prefer their own
generations~\cite{panickssery2024selfpref}, which motivates judging with a model from a different
family and validating the judge against teachers. Cognitive levels are commonly expressed with the
revised Bloom taxonomy~\cite{anderson2001bloom}.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

\Mimir{} is a set of FastAPI microservices behind an Nginx gateway, with a React/TypeScript frontend
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). \emph{Wellspring} extracts text from PDF, DOCX and plain-text files.
\emph{RuneCarver} splits it into semantic chunks: sentences are embedded with
multilingual-E5~\cite{wang2024me5}, and cuts are placed where the cosine distance between
neighbouring sentences exceeds a percentile (or mean\,+\,$k\sigma$) threshold, within a target
chunk size. \emph{Bifrost} embeds and indexes the chunks in Qdrant, retrieves context and runs the
generation job, streaming from the university GenAI server or a local Ollama model. \emph{Heimdall}
validates the output schema and scores quality. \emph{The Forge} orchestrates background work and
keeps a metadata-only audit log. \emph{Skald} renders the reviewed exam as PDF or Moodle XML.

\textbf{Hardware target.} The whole stack, including a 7B generator (Qwen2.5-7B~\cite{qwen25} in
Ollama) and the E5 encoder, is sized for an 8\,GB consumer GPU; the embedding models run on CPU in
float32. Generation jobs are asynchronous: \texttt{POST /generate} returns a job id, and
\texttt{GET /status} reports the stage (retrieving, generating, validating), the progress derived
from the length of the streamed output, and a remaining-time estimate blended from the observed pace
and the median duration of recent jobs, kept separately for local and server models. The frontend
turns this into a step list, a progress bar and a countdown, so a teacher waiting one or two minutes
knows what is happening.

\textbf{Planner.} One LLM call proposes $n+3$ concepts that cover the document, each with the chunk
identifiers that support it and an importance score. Slots are filled greedily, preferring concepts
that touch chunks not yet covered, so the exam spreads over the whole document; the three spare
concepts serve as replacements. Each slot receives a question type and a Bloom
level~\cite{anderson2001bloom} drawn by largest-remainder allocation from a difficulty-dependent mix
(easy: remember/understand; medium: understand/apply/analyse; hard: apply/analyse/evaluate). The
requested count and types are therefore met by construction, not by prompting.

\textbf{Per-slot retrieval and generation.} For each slot the concept's own chunks are combined with
the top-$k$ search hits for the concept (dense E5 search, or BM25 and dense fused with
RRF~\cite{cormack2009rrf} in the hybrid arm). The generator writes \emph{one} question per call and
must cite the chunk identifiers that support the key.

\textbf{Verifier chain.} Checks run cheapest first: shape (four options with one key for MCQ),
meta-references (``according to the text\ldots''), valid citations, then an LLM check that the cited
chunks support the key and refute every distractor, and finally a \emph{blind answer test}: the
model answers the question from the cited text without seeing the key and must pick the keyed
option. A failed check is returned to the generator as feedback. After the retry budget the slot
switches to a spare concept; as a last resort the best attempt is kept and flagged
\texttt{verified=false}, so the teacher sees which questions need attention. Near-duplicates
(embedding similarity $>0.9$) are regenerated. Table~\ref{tab:verifier} lists the checks and
the feedback each one returns.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Verifier checks, in execution order. Cheap rule checks run first so that malformed
questions never cost an LLM call.}
\label{tab:verifier}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.1cm}c>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
Check & LLM & Feedback to the generator on failure \\
\midrule

\textbf{Concept graph (E3).} A GraphRAG-style~\cite{edge2024graphrag} variant extracts concepts,
definitions and typed relations per 3{,}000-character batch. Graph concepts replace the planner
call, sibling concepts (same parent, different fact) are offered as distractor ideas, and 1-hop
neighbour chunks join the context.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Blueprint generation for one exam}
\label{alg:blueprint}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require chunks $C$, count $n$, types $T$, difficulty $d$, retries $r$
\State $K \gets \textsc{Plan}(C, n+3)$ \Comment{concepts with chunk ids}
\State $S \gets \textsc{Allocate}(K, n, T, \textsc{BloomMix}(d))$
\ForAll{slot $s \in S$}
  \For{$a = 0 \ldots r$}
    \State $X \gets \textsc{Retrieve}(s.\mathit{concept}) \cup s.\mathit{chunks}$
    \State $q \gets \textsc{Generate}(s, X, \mathit{feedback})$
    \State $\mathit{feedback} \gets \textsc{Verify}(q, X)$ \Comment{shape, cites, grounding, blind}
    \If{$\mathit{feedback} = \emptyset \wedge \neg\textsc{Dup}(q)$} accept $q$; \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{not accepted} try a spare concept, else keep best $q$ as unverified \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return questions ordered easy $\rightarrow$ hard, with citations
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Privacy and Trust by Design}
\label{sec:privacy}

\Mimir{} is built for use under the GDPR~\cite{gdpr2016} and the transparency and human-oversight
duties of the EU AI Act~\cite{aiact2024}.

\textbf{Zero retention.} Uploaded bytes, extracted text, chunks, embeddings and prompts exist only in
service memory for the duration of a job; Bifrost clears the vector store when a job ends or fails,
and job results expire after 60 minutes. The audit log stores the job id, model, prompt version,
quality score and SHA-256 fingerprints and lengths of the request and context---never content. Access
logs keep shortened IP addresses and no query strings. Nothing from uploads is backed up.

\textbf{Processing location.} A \texttt{LOCAL\_ONLY} switch disables every external model call.
Otherwise the upload screen names where the document will be processed, a model selector groups
models into ``university server'' and ``local'', and server models carry a ``data leaves this
server'' badge. The upload requires an explicit confirmation that the user may process the
document and that it contains no special-category or student data.

\textbf{Human oversight and transparency.} Generated questions open in an editor (reorder, edit,
change the key, regenerate one question, view the cited source passage) and cannot be exported
without approval. The exported PDF states that the content was produced with an AI assistant and
names the model. Saving a test is opt-in, with a 12-month maximum retention. A compliance pack
(record of processing, retention schedule, DPIA screening, breach procedure) with a draft
data-processing-agreement template and a legitimate-interest assessment is maintained with the code.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval}

The harness (\texttt{tests/eval}) runs in three steps: \emph{run} generates exams for every
document$\times$seed of an arm, \emph{score} computes metrics and calls the judge, and \emph{analyze}
/ \emph{report} produce tables, tests and figures. Each run stores its resolved configuration, git
commit and dataset version; judge answers are cached, so re-scoring is free.

\textbf{Dataset.} Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the 39 documents. EduQG~\cite{hadifar2023eduqg}
and MILQA~\cite{novak2023milqa} importers pick short, medium and long documents and keep only
reference questions whose evidence sentences occur in the document; reference questions are used
for gold similarity and retrieval recall, not as targets. The dataset is frozen as \texttt{eval-v1}
before the full run.

\textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Format compliance} is the share of exams with exactly the requested number
and shape of questions; an unparsable output or Bifrost's hard-coded error exam counts as a failed
exam, while infrastructure errors are excluded and retried. The judge scores, per question,
\emph{grounding} (the source supports the key, with a quoted sentence), \emph{distractor validity}
(each distractor is false and on topic) and four 1--5 rubric scores (correctness, clarity, distractor
quality, Bloom fit); in a second call it answers the question \emph{blind} from the source, giving
\emph{blind answerability}. Automatic metrics add the meta-reference rate, duplicate rate, section
coverage, similarity to the reference questions, retrieval recall@$k$ and MRR against the gold
evidence sentences, and cost (time, LLM calls, peak VRAM). The \emph{quality index} is the mean of
the four measures every arm has: format compliance, grounding, blind answerability and distractor
validity.

\textbf{Judge.} The judge is gpt-oss-120b~\cite{gptoss2025}, a different model family from the Qwen
generators, to avoid self-preference~\cite{panickssery2024selfpref}; the harness warns when judge and
generator share a family. It sees the chunks each question was written from (or the retrieved
context for E0) and runs with low reasoning effort to keep a full run tractable.

\textbf{Statistics.} Documents are the unit of analysis: metrics are averaged over seeds and
questions per document. We report means with 10{,}000-resample bootstrap 95\,\%
CIs~\cite{efron1993}, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945} over documents with Holm
correction~\cite{holm1979} for the key steps (E1--E0, E2--E1, E3--E2, E4--E0), and the matched-pairs
rank-biserial correlation~\cite{kerby2014} as effect size. Fewer than six paired documents can never
reach $p<0.05$ with this test, so pilot runs are labelled as such by the report generator.

\textbf{Teacher rating.} Blind rating sheets sample 15 questions per arm round-robin over
documents, shuffled per rater, without arm labels, using the same rubric as the judge plus a
``would use'' item. Agreement between teachers is measured with Krippendorff's
$\alpha$~\cite{krippendorff2004}; judge--teacher agreement with Spearman's $\rho$ per criterion and
Cohen's $\kappa$ for judge ``grounded'' versus teacher correctness $\geq 4$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Diagnostic findings}

Building the harness produced two findings that change how earlier measurements must be read.

\textbf{Fallback exams counted as results.} When every model call fails, Bifrost returns a
hard-coded one-question exam asking the user to retry. An end-to-end test run on 3 May 2026 reported
the generation step for all three pilot documents as successful in 4.0--9.0\,s, but each ``exam''
was this fallback (one question instead of the requested four). Because the payload is valid JSON of
the right schema, latency and schema checks alone could not reveal it. The fallback is now marked
\texttt{is\_fallback}, the test client flags it, and the harness counts it as a failed exam.

\textbf{Chunker truncation.} RuneCarver embedded the whole document in one encoder pass with a
512-token limit; every sentence after roughly the first page received a zero vector, so cut points
beyond that were driven by the size limit, not topic changes. At 4.0--4.5 characters per token the
window covers about 2{,}000--2{,}300 characters, which means that on average an estimated
68--71\,\% of each dataset document's text (median 71--74\,\%) was chunked without semantic signal,
and 31--33 of the 39 documents lost more than half. The encoder now works in $\leq$512-token
windows (identical output on short texts; covered by a unit test); arm E5a keeps the old encoder to
measure the effect. Fig.~\ref{fig:lengths} shows how far the dataset documents extend past the
old encoder window.

\subsection{Implementation and quality assurance}

The evaluation harness has 32 unit and end-to-end tests; the end-to-end tests run the full
run$\to$score$\to$rate$\to$analyze$\to$report flow with fake services and a mock LLM, so the
statistics and report tables are tested without a GPU. The job tracker behind the progress display
and the model endpoints are covered by service tests with a fake clock. The frontend is typed
against an OpenAPI contract and has 48 component and contract tests and 17 browser end-to-end
tests, including an automated accessibility scan (axe) in each of the 12 colour-palette and
light/dark combinations; a CI script checks WCAG~AA contrast for all of them, and a key-parity
check keeps the Hungarian and English interface strings (669 keys) in sync. These checks keep the
system usable while the evaluation changes the pipeline underneath it.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{Measurement before modelling.} The two diagnostic findings are the most transferable lesson.
Both defects produced plausible artefacts---a fast, schema-valid response and a normal-looking
chunking---and would have biased any comparison built on them. Explicit failure states (a flagged
fallback, per-stage status), before/after arms for every fix and a harness with end-to-end tests
with fake services and a mock LLM made them visible.

\textbf{Judge validity.} An LLM judge is a proxy. We mitigate self-preference by using another model
family, anchor grounding with a quoted evidence sentence, and validate the judge against blind
teacher ratings; if agreement is low, the teacher ratings take precedence. Showing the judge only the
question's own context makes it faster but can miss contradictions elsewhere in the document.

\textbf{Scope.} The questions are multiple choice; true/false and open questions are supported by the
system but not evaluated here. Documents are the unit of analysis, so 39 documents limit the power
for small effects, and the server arms use a 12-document subset. Wikipedia and OpenStax text is
cleaner than typical lecture notes. The Hungarian and English subsets come from different sources,
so language effects are confounded with source. Finally, real authentication and per-user ownership
checks are still in development; until then the service is operated for a closed user group.

\section{Conclusion}

\Mimir{} shows that exam generation from teachers' own material can be structured, verifiable and
privacy-preserving on commodity hardware: a blueprint guarantees the requested exam, a verifier
chain checks every question against its cited source, uploaded content is not retained, and a
teacher approves every question before use. Its evaluation protocol---bilingual data with reference
questions, single-factor arms, a cross-family judge validated by teachers, and document-level
statistics---already caught two defects that would have invalidated a naive comparison. The code,
configurations and dataset manifests are released with the system so that the comparison can be
reproduced and extended.
