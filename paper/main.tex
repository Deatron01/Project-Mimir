\begin{abstract}
The simplest way to obtain an exam from teaching material today is to upload the document to a
chat-based large language model (LLM) and ask for a test. This gives no evidence that the keyed
answers are supported by the document, no control over which parts of the material are covered,
and it sends the material to a third party. We present \Mimir{}, an open system that generates
multiple-choice exams from a teacher's own documents with a small language model running on a
consumer GPU. \Mimir{} replaces the single prompt by an explicit method: semantic chunking, a
\emph{blueprint} that assigns concepts, question types and Bloom levels to slots, per-question
generation with chunk citations, and a verifier chain that checks grounding, distractors and blind
answerability before a mandatory human review. Uploaded content is kept only for the duration of a
job. We also contribute an evaluation methodology: ten experiment arms that switch components on and
off, a 39-document Hungarian and English dataset with 326 reference questions, a cross-family LLM
judge and document-level statistics. A pilot on one Hungarian document shows that planning improved
grounding, blind answerability and distractor validity of the local 7B model and raised section
coverage from 50\,\% to 58\,\%, that a large server model scored higher but drew its questions from
only 25\,\% of the document's sections, that the local model occupies 5.4--5.9\,GB of GPU memory, that a verifier run by the same 7B model
does not yet separate good from bad questions, and that questions whose stem already contains the key
inflate both automatic and LLM-judge scores. Run-to-run variation alone reaches 0.07 on the quality
index, which is why the main study uses 39 documents.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Writing good multiple-choice questions is slow: the stem must be unambiguous, exactly one option must
be correct according to the course material, and the distractors must be plausible but provably
wrong. Large language models (LLMs) can draft such questions~\cite{kurdi2020systematic,alhazmi2024distractor},
and the most common way teachers use them is direct: upload the lecture notes to a chat assistant and
ask for ``ten multiple-choice questions''. This is convenient, but it has four weaknesses that matter
in a school.

\textbf{(P1) Correctness is not checked.} Nothing verifies that the keyed option is supported by the
document or that the distractors are actually false; LLMs produce plausible but unsupported
statements, which self-verification can reduce but not remove~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine,dhuliawala2024cove}.
\textbf{(P2) Structure and coverage are not controlled.} The requested number, types and difficulty
of questions are only suggested in the prompt, and long inputs are used unevenly---models attend to
the beginning and end of long contexts more than to the middle~\cite{liu2024lost}---so questions
cluster on a few sections. \textbf{(P3) Privacy.} Teaching material may contain personal data, and a
hosted assistant makes the provider a recipient of it. \textbf{(P4) Dependence on large hosted models.}
Whole documents need long contexts and large models; the small models a school can run itself have
limited context and memory.

\Mimir{} addresses these with a method rather than a prompt, and runs it on a single consumer GPU.
We ask three research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Does structured generation---planning, per-question grounding and
verification---produce more valid and better-covering exams than prompting a model with the document?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] What does each component contribute, and what does it cost in time and model calls?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] How close does a 7B model on an 8\,GB consumer GPU come to a large server-hosted model?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) The \Mimir{} generation method: semantic chunking, a coverage-driven
exam blueprint, cited per-question generation and a verifier chain (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
(2) A privacy-preserving local deployment with zero retention of uploaded content and a measured
GPU footprint that fits an 8\,GB card (Section~\ref{sec:privacy}). (3) An evaluation methodology and
open harness: ten single-factor arms, a bilingual dataset with reference questions, a cross-family
judge and document-level statistics (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). (4) Pilot findings, including two
measurement defects that would have invalidated earlier results and an estimate of run-to-run
noise (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Question and distractor generation.} Surveys of educational question
generation~\cite{kurdi2020systematic} and distractor generation~\cite{alhazmi2024distractor} note that
evaluation often relies on reference-overlap metrics or small human studies that rarely verify answer
correctness. Small models can produce comparable educational questions after further pre-training
and fine-tuning~\cite{fawzi2023small,fawzi2024towards}, and small private models have been compared
with large ones for Bloom-aligned assessment design~\cite{jaldi2026small}. We use off-the-shelf models
without fine-tuning and add explicit grounding and verification.

\textbf{Retrieval and long contexts.} Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds output in retrieved
passages~\cite{lewis2020rag}; hybrid retrieval fuses BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25} with dense
embeddings~\cite{wang2024me5} using reciprocal rank fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf}, and graph-based
RAG builds a concept graph for questions that need global context~\cite{edge2024graphrag}. Long-context
prompting, the alternative, uses the middle of the input poorly~\cite{liu2024lost}.

\textbf{Verification and small models.} Iterative self-feedback~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine} and
verification questions~\cite{dhuliawala2024cove} reduce factual errors, and spending inference compute
can substitute for model size~\cite{snell2025scaling}. Quantised 4-bit models lose little accuracy on
general benchmarks~\cite{li2024evalquant,kurtic2025bf16}, which makes local deployment plausible.

\textbf{LLM judges and languages.} LLM judges correlate with humans~\cite{zheng2023judge} but favour
their own family~\cite{panickssery2024selfpref}. For Hungarian, benchmarks~\cite{ligetinagy2024hulu}
and QA data~\cite{novak2023milqa} exist, but we know of no evaluation of exam generation.

\textbf{Document processing.} Text is extracted from PDF, DOCX or plain text and split into semantic
chunks: sentences are embedded with multilingual E5~\cite{wang2024me5}, and a cut is placed where the
cosine distance between neighbouring sentences exceeds a percentile (or mean\,+\,$k\sigma$) threshold,
within a target size. The encoder works in windows of at most 512 tokens (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).
Chunks are indexed in a vector store that exists only for the current job.

\textbf{Blueprint.} One call proposes $n+3$ concepts that cover the document, each with the chunk
identifiers that support it. Slots are filled greedily, preferring concepts that touch chunks no slot
covers yet, so the exam spreads over the document; the three spare concepts serve as replacements.
Each slot receives a question type and a Bloom level~\cite{anderson2001bloom} by largest-remainder
allocation from a difficulty-dependent mix (easy: remember/understand; medium:
understand/apply/analyse; hard: apply/analyse/evaluate). Count and types are therefore guaranteed by
construction (G2).

\textbf{Grounded generation.} For each slot, the concept's chunks are combined with the top-$k$
retrieved passages (dense, or BM25 and dense fused with RRF), and the model writes \emph{one}
question that must cite the chunk identifiers supporting its key (G1). A concept-graph variant (E3)
extracts concepts and typed relations per 3{,}000-character batch, replaces the planner call with graph
concepts, offers sibling concepts as distractor ideas, and adds 1-hop neighbour chunks to the context.

\textbf{Verification.} Checks run cheapest first (Table~\ref{tab:verifier}). A failed check returns its
reason to the generator for one retry; after that the slot moves to a spare concept, and as a last
resort the best attempt is kept and flagged as unverified for the teacher. Near-duplicates (embedding
similarity $>0.9$) are regenerated. After the pilot we added leakage checks, a repair call for malformed
last-resort questions, and an optional verifier from another model family.

\textbf{Human review.} The draft opens in an editor where the teacher can edit, reorder, change the
key, regenerate a single question and view each question's cited source passage. Nothing is exported
without approval, and the exported PDF states that the content was drafted with an AI system and by
which model (G5).

\section{Privacy and Local Deployment}
\label{sec:privacy}

\textbf{Zero retention (G3).} Uploaded bytes, extracted text, chunks, embeddings and prompts exist only
in service memory for the duration of a job; the vector store is cleared when the job ends or fails,
and job results expire after 60 minutes. The audit log keeps the job identifier, model, prompt version,
quality score and SHA-256 fingerprints and lengths of the request and context---never content. A
\texttt{LOCAL\_ONLY} switch disables every external model call; otherwise the upload screen names where
the document will be processed and lets the teacher choose the local model.

\textbf{Consumer GPU (G4).} The generator is Qwen2.5-7B~\cite{qwen25} with 4-bit (Q4\_K\_M) weights in
the Ollama runtime, the only thing on the GPU; embeddings and all services run on the CPU. Because every
call in the blueprint pipeline is short, the context stays at 8{,}192--16{,}384 tokens; the 7B model's
grouped-query attention needs 56\,KiB of key--value cache per token, about 0.9\,GB at 16{,}384 tokens.
In the pilot, GPU memory rose from 1.6--1.9\,GB (desktop, model not yet loaded) to a peak of
7.0--7.8\,GB during generation, so the model and runtime occupied 5.4--5.9\,GB---within the budget of
an 8\,GB card, including the desktop. Table~\ref{tab:budget} estimates the budget for other local
models: 3--8B models at 4 bits fit 8\,GB; a 4-bit 14B model or an 8-bit 7B model needs 12\,GB.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:eval}

The harness (\texttt{tests/eval}) runs in three steps: \emph{run} generates exams for every
document$\times$seed of an arm, \emph{score} computes metrics and calls the judge, and \emph{report}
produces tables, tests and figures. Each run stores its configuration, git commit and dataset version.

\textbf{Dataset.} Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the 39 documents, frozen as \texttt{eval-v1}.
EduQG~\cite{hadifar2023eduqg} and MILQA~\cite{novak2023milqa} importers select short, medium and long
documents and keep reference questions whose evidence sentence occurs in the document; references are
used for retrieval recall and similarity, not as targets.

\textbf{Measures.} \emph{Format compliance} is 1 if an exam has exactly the requested number and shape
of questions. Per question, the judge decides \emph{grounding} (the source supports the key, with a
quoted sentence), \emph{distractor validity} (each distractor false and on topic) and \emph{blind
answerability} (answering from the source without the key picks the keyed option), and gives 1--5
rubric scores. The \emph{quality index} is the mean of these four rates. \emph{Coverage} is the share
of the document's chunks that are the closest chunk (by embedding) of at least one question; we also record duplicates,
meta-references, answer leakage, unverified questions, calls, tokens, time, peak GPU memory, the GPU
model and the share of the model held in GPU memory.

\textbf{Judge and teachers.} The judge is gpt-oss-120b~\cite{gptoss2025}, from a different model family
than the generators, to avoid self-preference~\cite{panickssery2024selfpref}. Blind teacher rating
sheets use the same rubric; Krippendorff's $\alpha$~\cite{krippendorff2004} and Spearman's $\rho$
validate the judge.

\textbf{Statistics.} Documents are the unit of analysis: measures are averaged over questions and seeds
per document, with 10{,}000-resample bootstrap CIs~\cite{efron1993}, paired Wilcoxon
tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945} with Holm correction~\cite{holm1979}, and rank-biserial effect
sizes~\cite{kerby2014}. Fewer than six documents cannot reach $p<0.05$, so single-document results are
reported descriptively.

\subsection{Measurement validity}
\label{sec:validity}

Building the harness exposed two defects that had made earlier end-to-end measurements invalid.
\emph{Fallback exams counted as results:} when every model call failed, the backend returned a
hard-coded one-question exam; a test run on 3 May 2026 reported all three documents as generated in
4--9\,s, but each ``exam'' was this placeholder. It is now flagged and counted as a failure.
\emph{Truncated chunking:} the chunker embedded each document in one 512-token pass, so every sentence
after roughly the first page received a zero vector. At 4.0--4.5 characters per token an estimated
68--71\,\% of the average \texttt{eval-v1} document was affected. The encoder now works in windows;
arm E5a keeps the old one to measure the effect. A third issue, answer leakage that inflates judge
scores, was found in the pilot (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\textbf{Run-to-run noise.} E0 and E5a received identical chunks, identical retrieved context and the
same seeds---the legacy and windowed encoders coincide on a document shorter than 512 tokens---yet
produced different questions and indices of 0.83 and 0.90. GPU inference is not bit-reproducible even
at low temperature, so on a single document differences below about 0.07 are noise. This is the main
reason the study uses 39 documents and document-level statistics.

\textbf{Planning helps (RQ1, RQ2).} Compared with naive RAG, the blueprint (E1) improved grounding
(67$\to$73\,\%), blind answerability (85$\to$93\,\%) and distractor validity (80$\to$87\,\%) at unchanged
format compliance, raised coverage from 50 to 58\,\% and the judge's correctness score from 3.5 to 3.7,
at 23 calls and 3.0 minutes per exam instead of one call and 20 seconds.

\textbf{Verification with the same small model is not yet effective (RQ2).} The verifier arms
flagged 3.3--6.0 of 10 questions per exam as unverified. In one of the three exams of each of E2, E2f,
E2h and E3, every attempt for a slot failed and the kept last-resort question had only two or three
options; this set format compliance to zero for that exam and lowered its quality index by 0.25.
Verification on top of planning did not raise grounding (73\,\% for E1 and E2) and lowered blind
answerability (93$\to$77\,\%). The concept-graph arm (E3) reached the highest local grounding (87\,\%), blind
answerability (100\,\%) and judge correctness (4.4 of 5, against 3.5 for E0), but these scores are
partly an artefact (see answer leakage below). Table~\ref{tab:verifier-judge} shows why: pooled over the four verifier arms, the questions the 7B
verifier passed were judged grounded \emph{less} often (66\,\%) than those it flagged (75\,\%); they were
blind-answerable somewhat more often (89 vs.\ 79\,\%), and the judge would use both groups equally often.
Scored as a classifier of the judge's grounding verdict, the verifier was at chance level in every arm
(Cohen's $\kappa$ from $-0.14$ to $0.07$, recall 0.42--0.62). A verifier that is
the generator itself is not independent of it~\cite{panickssery2024selfpref}, and a 7B model cannot
yet decide reliably whether a passage entails a key. The pilot thus identified two fixes for the main
study: the last-resort path must never emit a malformed question, and the verifier must be validated
as a classifier---its agreement with the judge and the teachers is measured, and a stronger or
different-family verifier is tested before its flag is shown to teachers.

\textbf{Answer leakage.} Reading the questions (Table~\ref{tab:examples}) exposed a failure that
neither our metrics nor the judge penalised: some verified questions copy a source sentence into the
stem and repeat it as the key. For keys of at least three words, the key's words appeared in the stem
($\ge$80\,\% token overlap) in 20\,\% of E3's questions, 7--10\,\% of E2f and E2h, 3\,\% of E2 and none
of the single-call or E1 questions (Table~\ref{tab:pilot}, Leak.), and 47\,\% of E3's stems were
statements rather than questions (10--33\,\% in five other arms). Such items are trivially
``grounded'' and ``blind-answerable'', and the judge still gave them 5 of 5 for correctness. The
verifier's feedback loop seems to push the small model towards copying the source verbatim, which is
the cheapest way to pass a grounding check. The verifier now has two rule checks for this (the key
must not be contained in the stem; the stem must be a question; Table~\ref{tab:verifier}), and the
leakage rate is reported as a metric.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why a method rather than a prompt.} The pilot supports the design argument on three points:
explicit planning improved grounding, blind answerability, distractor validity and coverage of
the same 7B model; a much larger model given the document still concentrated on one part of it; and
per-question citations let a reader---the judge here, the teacher in practice---check each key against
its source, which exposed both the naive baseline's wrong keys and the verified arms' leakage. It also shows the limits: verification multiplies calls by
60--100, and a verifier that is the same 7B model adds cost without yet adding information. The
component that must earn its place in the main study is therefore the verifier, not the blueprint.

\textbf{From pilot to main study.} Each pilot finding changes the design of the \texttt{eval-v1}
run on the 39 documents (items (i)--(iv) are implemented and tested in the harness): (i) a \emph{whole-document} baseline---the document in one prompt, locally and
on the server---tests P1 and P2 directly instead of through naive RAG; (ii) the last-resort path keeps
only well-formed questions; (iii) two rule checks reject questions whose stem contains the key or is
not a question, and the leakage rate is reported; (iv) the verifier is evaluated as a classifier
against the judge and the teachers, and a verifier from a different model family is compared with the
self-verifier; (v) three teachers rate a blind sample so that conclusions do not rest on the LLM judge
alone; and (vi) every arm is run on all documents, so that differences of the size the pilot shows can
be separated from the 0.07 run-to-run noise. Its results will replace Table~\ref{tab:pilot}:
\tbd{[quality index per arm with 95\,\% CIs; paired tests for E1--E0, E2--E1, E3--E2, E4--E0; leakage
rates; verifier and judge agreement with teachers]}.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} Every run stores its resolved configuration, git commit, dataset version and
generator settings; judge answers are cached by a hash of model, prompt version and prompt, so
re-scoring is deterministic; runs resume after interruptions; and the full
run$\to$score$\to$report flow is covered by end-to-end tests with fake services and a mock LLM. The
report also writes the paper's tables (Tables~\ref{tab:pilot} and~\ref{tab:verifier-judge}). The
pilot's raw exams, judge verdicts and report are released with the code.

\textbf{Limitations.} The pilot covers one short Hungarian document on which naive RAG sees three of
four chunks, the setting least favourable to planning; long documents should show larger differences.
The judge sees each question's cited context and may miss contradictions elsewhere. Only
multiple-choice questions are evaluated. The Hungarian and English subsets differ in source, so
language effects are confounded with source.

\section{Conclusion}

\Mimir{} turns exam generation from a single prompt into a verifiable method that runs on a consumer
GPU without retaining teaching material: a blueprint guarantees the requested exam and spreads it
over the document, every question cites its source and is checked before a teacher approves it, and a
7B model fits in 6\,GB of GPU memory. A pilot shows that planning improves a small model's grounding,
distractors and coverage, that a large model given the document still clusters its questions, that a
same-model verifier and an LLM judge can both be fooled by questions that copy their answer into the
stem, and that single-document comparisons are dominated by run-to-run noise. The bilingual 39-document study with teacher ratings
tests these findings; code, configurations and dataset manifests are released for reproduction.
